# American Intonation — The System, and What To Do

**Notation:** CAPS = the strong syllable · `ə` = the lazy "uh", short and vague · ↘ ↗ = pitch falls or rises, *starting on the CAPS syllable*.
So `də-VEL-əp-mənt` reads: mush, **STRONG**, mush, mush.

---

# 1. Why you have an accent

English has no gaps between words. So how does an American find where one word ends and the next begins?

They guess, using one shortcut: **a strong syllable means a new word is starting.** It works because most English words begin strong.

Now say *development* the French way — four clear syllables, roughly equal length. To their ear, that's four word-beginnings. The shortcut returns nonsense and they fall back on slow, effortful parsing.

**That's your accent.** Not your R, not your TH. You're handing your listener four words where you meant one. It's also exactly why people say *"his English is perfect, but he's tiring to listen to."*

Which gives you the one sentence everything else depends on:

> **English stress isn't something you add to a syllable. It's a contrast you create by destroying the syllables around it.**

You already hit the right syllable much of the time. You never destroy the neighbours. That's the job.

**The other half of the problem:** French puts its emphasis at the **end of a phrase**. English puts it on a **specific syllable inside a specific word**, usually mid-phrase. Your melody is fine — it's anchored in the wrong place. That's what people hear as "French sing-song."

Two problems: **timing** and **anchoring**. Everything below fixes one or the other.

---

# 2. The whole system, on one sentence

> *I think the development team's estimate is probably a bit optimistic.*

**What you probably say now:** every syllable full and roughly equal, with a rise at the end. Twenty-odd audible beats where an American gives six.

**Layer 1 — destroy the weak syllables.**
`də-VEL-əp-mənt`. `estimate` as a noun is `ES-tə-mət` — the ending is mush, not "mate". `probably` → `PROB-ly`, a whole syllable vanishes. `the, is, a` → `thə, əz, ə`.

**Layer 2 — stress where the word says.**
`deVELopment`, not `developMENT`. `ESTimate`, not `estiMATE`.

**Layer 3 — one word carries the point.**
Each chunk gets exactly one main emphasis, and everything after it goes flat and low:

`I think the deVELopment team's EStəmət is PROBly a bit op-tə-MIS-tic`

And if you've already been discussing the estimate, it disappears entirely — because **English deletes emphasis on anything already mentioned**:

`I think it's PROBly a bit op-tə-MIS-tic`

**Layer 4 — choose a tune.**

`↘ op-tə-MIS-tic` — flat assertion. Reads as: *your estimate is wrong.*
`↘↗ op-tə-MIS-tic` — falls on **MIS**, floats back up across *-tic*. Reads as: *I have a reservation, and I'm leaving you room.*

Same words. Different meeting.

**Reduce → stress → emphasize → tune.** Plus ear training, which has to come first. Five stages.

---

# 3. The five stages

Each ends in a gate — a test you run on yourself. Don't skip ahead; that's the main way people lose a year.

## Stage 0 — Get your ear working
*Two weeks of focus, then 5 minutes a day forever.*

Every drill in existence says "compare yourself to a native model." That's useless if you can't hear the difference — and there's a well-documented finding that French speakers specifically struggle to *store* stress information, even when they hear it in the moment. Skip this stage and you'll practise diligently for six months against a standard you can't perceive, consolidating your errors. That's the most common way this fails.

**Do:** build a scored quiz. Take 60 words you're unsure of. Get 3–4 different voices for each (Forvo, YouGlish, a couple of TTS voices). Shuffle words *and* voices. For each clip, answer one question: **which syllable is stressed?** Check immediately. **Write your score down.** 15 minutes most days — any audio flashcard app does this.

> **Gate:** 85% or better, shuffled voices, two sessions in a row.

## Stage 1 — Make weak syllables genuinely weak
*The biggest single win. You'll live here a while.*

First, four things you physically need — about five hours, once, then done:

- **A real schwa.** Not the French *deux/peur* vowel — that one's rounded and too clear, and Americans hear it as a **full** vowel, i.e. as a new word. Say "uh" like a shrug, lips completely relaxed. *Check in a mirror: if your lips move forward, it's wrong.*
- **The American -er** (`better, manager, computer`). Say "uh", then pull your tongue back and slightly up until the sound darkens. No French R scrape — a weak syllable with friction in it isn't weak.
- **Syllabic consonants.** `button` = `BUT-n`. Never open your mouth after the T. Say `BUT-ən` and you've turned two syllables into three beats.
- **Unreleased final consonants.** Say `stop` and freeze with your lips shut. That little trailing `-ə` you add after final T/P/K lands exactly where English wants a clean ending.

**Do:** build outward from the beat, never left to right — `TAH → TAH-grəf → fə-TAH-grə-fi`. Then the metronome test at 90 bpm: say `DOGS EAT BONES` and `The dogs would have EATen all of the BONES` in the **same total time**. Three beats each; 3 syllables versus 12.

Exaggerate everything to 300%. Then, once, record what feels like a ridiculous parody of an American and play it back — it will sound like a mild, pleasant accent. That single discovery changes how hard you're willing to push.

> **Gate:** record yourself saying *administration*. Measure your longest and shortest syllable. **Ratio of 3:1 or better.** Americans run 3:1 to 5:1; you're probably at 1.5:1.

## Stage 2 — Fix the stress on your own vocabulary
*Fast — weeks, not months. Start here for morale.*

This is knowledge, not motor skill, so it moves quickly.

**Do:** record yourself reading a page of your actual work writing. Check every word of 3+ syllables against a US dictionary with audio. Every miss goes on a running list in your notes app, reviewed weekly. **That list is worth more than any generic table.**

Four rules cover most professional vocabulary:

- **The `-ion` family** (`-ion, -ial, -ious, -ual, -ian`): stress the syllable immediately before it. `adminiSTRAtion`, `esSENtial`, `poliTIcian`. One rule, thousands of words.
- **`-ic`, `-ity`:** same. `ecoNOMic`, `aBILity`.
- **`-ate` verbs, `-logy`, `-graphy`:** stress **two** syllables before. `CALculate`, `phoTOgraphy`.
- **`-ary/-ory/-ery`:** stress hard left, mush everything after — `NEsəseri`, `LABrətori`. Your worst minefield, because every French equivalent is late-stressed.

And one heuristic that saves you constantly: **if a word looks French and you don't know the stress, guess earlier than feels natural.** The French pull is always rightward.

> **Gate:** dictate a paragraph of your own writing to your phone's speech recognition. It should transcribe your long words correctly. Systematic misfires point straight at systematic stress errors — free, blunt, unbiased.

## Stage 3 — Put the emphasis where you mean it
*Where "understood" becomes "understood correctly."*

One chunk, one main emphasis — and **everything after it goes flat and dead.** That second part is what French speakers find hardest, because your instinct is to hold your voice up until the final syllable and then drop.

**Do:** drill long tails until they stay at the floor.

> It's about the ↘MANagement. / That's the ↘DIFficulty. / It was comPLETEly ↘UNnecessary.

This one drill removes most of the sing-song impression.

Then learn that moving the emphasis changes the sentence entirely:

> ↘I didn't say he stole it. *(someone else said it)*
> I didn't ↘SAY he stole it. *(I implied it)*
> I didn't say he ↘STOLE it. *(he borrowed it)*

Six placements, six meanings, one string of words. **In French you'd do this with syntax** — *ce n'est pas moi qui l'ai dit*. English does it with pitch and assumes you will too. So you produce flawless English in which your actual point goes unmarked. In a negotiation, that's expensive.

And delete the emphasis on old information — this is grammar, not manners:

> *"Where's the report?"* → "I ↘LEFT it on your desk." ✓
> not "I left the report on your ↘DESK." ✗

> **Gate:** produce all six placements of *I didn't say he stole it* on demand, and state what each one means.

## Stage 4 — Melody
*Six tunes. Six well-controlled beats twelve half-controlled.*

| Tune | Feels like | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Fall** ↘ | done, committed | statements, commands, **wh-questions** |
| **Low rise** ↗ | genuine question | yes/no questions |
| **Fall-rise** ↘↗ | "…but" | hedging, doubt, polite disagreement |
| **Rise-fall** ↗↘ | strong, impressed | emphasis, contrast |
| **High rise** ↗ | "I'm not finished" | continuation, storytelling |
| **Flat** → | routine, bored | lists, transactions |

**Two quick wins, do them this week.** Wh-questions **fall** — `Where are you ↘GOing?` In French they rise, and rising in English makes you sound permanently unsure. And **falls must reach the floor**; stopping mid-range reads as evasive or unfinished.

**The one that matters professionally is the fall-rise.** Without it you have exactly two options — flat assertion or question. So every reservation you have comes out as a flat assertion, and that is precisely why competent non-native professionals get read as blunt or cold.

> "That won't ↘WORK." → dismissive
> "I'm not sure that'll ↘↗WORK." → a normal colleague

Produce it: start mid, jump up on the strong syllable, drop, then float back up. Trace it with your hand. It'll feel theatrical — do it too big for a few weeks and it normalizes.

**And know this:** enthusiasm in English is carried by **pitch**, not word choice. "That's great" with a narrow range means "fine, whatever." If colleagues think you're lukewarm about things you're excited about, that's why.

> **Gate:** say *You're going to Paris* with all six tunes, and state what each one means.

# 4. How to practise

**30 minutes daily genuinely beats 3.5 hours on Sunday** — motor skills consolidate during sleep, so spacing does something massing can't.

```
 5 min   ear quiz, scored, written down
 5 min   warm-up drill for your current stage
15 min   current stage: chorus with a model → shadow it → say it alone
 5 min   record a 2-min monologue. Review 30 seconds. Check ONE thing.
```

**Four rules that matter more than any exercise:**

**1. One thing per session.** You can consciously monitor roughly *one* dimension at a time. Name it out loud before you start — "today, only vowel reduction" — and let everything else be bad.

**2. Drill it, then scramble it.** Reps to find the movement, then **mix it randomly** with other material. People who only drill in blocks are flawless in practice and revert the instant they're in a real conversation. This is the number one reason pronunciation work fails to transfer.

**3. Predict before you check.** Record yourself, **write down what you think went wrong**, and only then compare to the model. Judging first is what trains your ear.

**4. Climb the ladder for every new thing.** Chorus → shadow → from memory → marked-up reading → plain reading → retelling → prepared monologue → 2 min spontaneous → real conversation. Every new feature starts at the bottom.

**Two things to actively avoid.** **Slowing down to be clearer** — it backfires, because it stretches your *weak* syllables and flattens the exact contrast clarity depends on. And **suppressing your melody** because someone said you sound sing-song — don't reduce the movement, *relocate* it.

**Timeline:** word stress moves in weeks · careful speech, 1–3 months · spontaneous speech, 4–8 months of daily work · how people perceive you, 6–18 months.

---

# 5. The part that isn't technical

Doing this properly will feel like pretending, and a wide American pitch range will feel theatrical — even slightly undignified — to a French sensibility. Most people hold back for exactly this reason, and it's the invisible ceiling on otherwise diligent practice.

Frame it as **a role, not a replacement.** You're adding a register, not deleting your French self — bilinguals routinely have a different voice in each language. Practise as a character first, privately, deliberately over the top. The physical skills transfer fine, and performance mode carries no self-consciousness.

Your target — **completely clear, native-like rhythm, still audibly French** — isn't a compromise. It's the best goal available, and far more achievable than passing for American.
