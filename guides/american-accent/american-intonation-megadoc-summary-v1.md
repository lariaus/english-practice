# American Pronunciation — The Short Version

## The diagnosis

Your problem isn't your sounds. It's **rhythm and where you put the emphasis**. Americans understand you fine — it just costs them effort. That's what "strong accent" means here.

**Why rhythm beats sounds:** English has no gaps between words, so listeners guess boundaries with a shortcut — *a strong syllable means a new word starts here*. When all your syllables are equally long with full clear vowels, **every syllable looks like a new word.** Their shortcut returns garbage and they parse the slow way. That's the "his English is perfect but he's tiring to listen to" feeling, precisely located.

So: weak syllables aren't sloppiness. **Every schwa you don't swallow is a fake word boundary you hand your listener.**

### Two root problems

**A — Timing.** Syllables too equal, vowels too full. Americans run a 3:1 to 5:1 length ratio between the longest and shortest syllable in a word. You're probably at 1.5:1.

**B — Anchoring.** French puts the emphasis at the *end of the phrase*. English puts it on a *specific syllable inside a specific word*, usually mid-phrase. You have the melody — it's attached in the wrong place. That's the "French sing-song."

> **English stress isn't something you add. It's a contrast you create by destroying the neighbours.** You push on the stressed syllable but skip the second half of the job.

## The 6 things worth your time, in order

### 1. Make weak syllables actually weak

Biggest single win. Americans identify the stressed syllable mostly by checking **whether the others collapsed** — not by pitch or volume. `development` = `dəVELəpmənt`: one long syllable, three mush.

Four prerequisites (~5 hours, once, early):

- **A real schwa.** Not French *deux*/*peur*. Lips neutral, tongue at rest, short and vague. If your lips move forward in a mirror, it's wrong.
- **American `-er`** (`better`, `computer`, `manager`). No /ʁ/ scrape. Say "uh", pull the tongue back and up until it darkens.
- **Syllabic consonants.** `button` = `BUT-n`, never `BUT-øn`. Straight from T into N, mouth never opens.
- **Don't release final consonants.** Say `stop`, freeze with lips shut. That little `-ə` after final T/P/K wrecks the rhythm exactly where English wants a clean ending.

**Drill:** say only the stressed syllable, loud and long. Then add the weak ones as fast and quiet as physically possible. `TAH… TAHgrəf… fəTAHgrəfi`. Build outward from the beat, never left-to-right.

### 2. Fix word stress on your work vocabulary

Fastest big gain — it's knowledge, so a few hundred words is a few weeks.

**The cognate trap.** Shared words, different stress, French always pulls right:

| English | French pull |
|---|---|
| im**POR**tant · de**VEL**opment | impor-TANT · développe-MENT |
| **COMF**tərbl (3 syll) · **NE**səseri | confor-TABLE · néces-SAIRE |
| **CA**təgori · **LAB**rətori | caté-GORIE · labora-TOIRE |
| **IM**ij · **MES**ij · **VIL**ij | i-MAGE · mes-SAGE · vil-LAGE |
| **DOC**yəmənt · **AR**gyəmənt · **EL**əmənt | docu-MENT · argu-MENT · élé-MENT |
| **CHAR**əctər · **AR**kətekt · **DIF**əkəlt | carac-TÈRE · archi-TECTE · diffi-CILE |

> **Heuristic: if a word looks French and you're unsure, guess earlier than feels natural.**

Rules worth learning:
- **`-ion` family** (`-ion, -ial, -ious, -ual, -ian`): stress the syllable right before. `adminiSTRAtion`, `esSENtial`. One rule, thousands of words.
- **`-ic, -ity`**: same. `ecoNOMic`, `aBILity`.
- **`-ate` verbs, `-logy`, `-graphy`**: two syllables before. `CALculate`, `phoTOgraphy`.
- **`-ary/-ory/-ery`**: stress far left, reduce everything after — the `necessary/secretary/temporary` minefield.
- **2-syllable nouns** → first. **2-syllable verbs** → second (`a RECord` / `to reCORD`).

Learn families so the shift feels normal: `PHOtograph → phoTOGrapher → photoGRAPHic → phoTOGraphy`.

**Ten-minute extras:** `thirTEEN` vs `THIRdy` (final stress + clear T vs initial + flapped T) — drill to death if you talk numbers. Initialisms stress the **last** letter: `C-E-O`, `A-P-I`, `K-P-I`.

### 3. Put the emphasis on the word that matters

Each chunk has exactly **one** main stress. Default: the last important content word. Move it and the meaning changes — *I didn't say he stole it* is six different sentences.

**French does this with syntax** (*c'est moi qui…*); **English does it with pitch and expects you to.** So you produce perfect English where your actual point goes unmarked. Expensive in a negotiation.

**And anything already mentioned loses its stress.** Grammar, not politeness.

- *Where's the report?* → "I **LEFT** it on your desk." ✅ not "I left the report on your **DESK**"
- *Do you like Boston?* → "I **LOVE** Boston." ✅ ("I love **BOS**ton" sounds like you're comparing cities)

Clean rule, easy fix: **already known = no stress.**

### 4. Drop the pitch on the stressed syllable, not at the end

*"It's about the **MAN**agement."* The fall happens on **MAN**; the next three syllables stay flat at the bottom. Your instinct is to hold your voice up and drop on the last syllable.

> It's about the **MAN**agement. / I talked to the **MAN**ager. / That's the **DIF**ficulty. / It was com**PLETE**ly unnecessary.

This one drill kills most of the sing-song impression.

**The edge case that breaks you:** you break specifically when a word's stress isn't final *and* the word sits at a phrase edge — `comPUter` becomes `compuTER` at the end of a question.

> The com**PU**ter | was the problem. / Did you check the com**PU**ter? ↗ / Is it im**POR**tant? ↗

You must rise to the top of your range on a final *unstressed* syllable while keeping the weight earlier. In French those always coincide. Highest-value single drill available to you.

### 5. Learn the fall-rise

| Tune | Feels like | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Fall** ↘ | committed, done | statements, commands, **wh-questions** |
| **Low rise** ↗ | genuine question | yes/no questions |
| **Fall-rise** ↘↗ | "…but" | hedging, doubt, polite disagreement |
| **Rise-fall** ↗↘ | strong, impressed | emphasis, contrast |
| **High rise** ↗ | "I'm not done" | continuation, narrative |
| **Flat** → | routine, bored | lists, transactions |

Quick wins: **wh-questions FALL** (`Where are you GOing?` ↘ — rising makes you sound permanently unsure), and **falls must reach the floor** (stopping mid-range reads as evasive; Americans commit to the bottom).

**Why the fall-rise matters most professionally:** it's how English speakers disagree, hedge and deliver bad news without saying so. Without it you have two options — flat assertion or question. Flat assertion where a fall-rise was expected is exactly why competent non-natives get read as blunt.

> "That won't **WORK**." ↘ = dismissive
> "I'm not sure that'll **WORK**." ↘↗ = normal human being

Produce it: start mid, jump up on the stressed syllable, drop, float back up. It'll feel theatrical. Overdo it.

**Also:** enthusiasm in English is *pitch*, not word choice. "That's great" with a narrow range means "fine, whatever."

### 6. Train your ears, with a written score

French speakers have a specific, documented handicap here: you hear stress in the moment but struggle to *store* it — so you can practice diligently against a standard you can't perceive, and consolidate errors.

**A third of your time on listening**, analytic not passive. You've had 10 years of passive.

Protocol: 60 words you're unsure of → 3–4 different speakers each (Forvo, YouGlish, TTS) → shuffle words *and* voices → guess which syllable is stressed → **check immediately** → **write the score down.** 15–20 min most days. The per-item feedback and the written score are what make it work.

## Pitch range

Your French range is probably normal — you're **suppressing** it in English because attention is eaten by words, grammar and sounds. Load problem, not capacity problem. So part of "widening your range" happens indirectly, by making everything else automatic. Check *where* the movement sits, not just how much.

---

## Connected speech

- **Weak forms**: `tə` not `tuː`, `ən` not `and`, `fɚ` not `for`, `kən` not `can`.
- **Contractions are obligatory**, not informal. "I am going" reads as emphatic or foreign.
- **Flapping**: T between vowels → quick D. `water` → `wadər`, `get it` → `gedit`, `a lot of` → `əlada`. Across word boundaries too. Most identifiable American feature.
- **`/nt/` reduction**: `twenty` → `tweny`, `interview` → `innerview`, `center` → `senner`.
- **Linking**: `pick it up` → `pi-ki-dup`. No glottal stops between vowels — glide: `go on` → `gowon`.
- **Deletion**: `chocolate` (3→2), `family` → `famli`, `probably` → `probly`, `different` → `diffrent`.

**Don't drill "gonna" as slang.** These appear *because* the rhythm produced them. Fix the rhythm and they come free.

## How to practice

**The rules matter more than the exercises.**

1. **One thing per session.** You can monitor about one dimension at a time. Name it first: "today, only vowel reduction." Everything else is allowed to be bad.
2. **Drill it, then mix it.** Reps to find the movement, then *interleave randomly*. People who only drill in blocks are perfect in practice and revert instantly in conversation — the #1 reason pronunciation practice fails to transfer.
3. **30 min daily beats 3.5 hours on Sunday.**
4. **Predict before you check.** Record, write what you think went wrong, *then* compare. That's what trains your ear.
5. **Exaggerate to 300%, then fade.** Verify once: record what feels like a ridiculous American parody and play it back — it'll sound like a mild, pleasant American accent. That changes how hard you're willing to push.
6. **Practice at the edge.** Comfortable = no longer training.

**Techniques, ranked:**

- **Chorusing** — speak *simultaneously* with a 3–8 sec loop, 10× round. Lowest effort, best for installing rhythm.
- **Shadowing** — repeat with a half-second lag. Most research behind it, specifically for prosody.
- **Humming the contour**, no words. Best melody exercise: isolates pitch from articulation.
- **Overlearned chunks** — drill one short phrase 50–100× over days until it's perfect with zero attention, then bank it. Build 50–100. An automatic chunk costs no attention, freeing your budget for everything else. Start with: your name and role · "nice to meet you" · "can you hear me okay?" · "let me share my screen" · "sorry, could you say that again?" · "does that make sense?"
- **Metronome** at 90 bpm — tap the beats, fit everything between. Can't fit it? Not reducing enough.
- **Rubber band** — stretch on stressed syllables. Makes duration physical.
- **Praat** (free) — your pitch track beside a native's. Differences you couldn't *hear* become impossible to miss. Set floor 60–75 Hz, ceiling 250–300 for a male voice, or you get garbage.

**The transfer ladder.** Everyone fails here: correct prosody when imitating is common, correct prosody unplanned is rare. Climb — chorusing → shadowing → from memory → marked-up reading → plain reading → retelling → prepared monologue → 2 min spontaneous with one focus → real conversation. Every new feature starts at rung 1; work two or three rungs at once.

**Keystone habit:** 2-minute daily monologue, recorded, random topic, same time. Review 30 seconds, check one thing.

## Skip this

**70% of your gain is reduction, word stress, and emphasis placement.** Another 20% is boundaries, the fall-rise, and range. The last 10% costs more than the first 90% combined.

**Don't bother:** vocal fry · compound stress rules · more than 6 tunes · regional US accents · uptalk · perfecting /r/ and /θ/ (accent features, not clarity features — a French R in rhythmically native speech is charming; a perfect American R in evenly-timed speech is not) · any app that gives a score without showing a pitch contour.

**Actively avoid:**

- **Speaking slowly to be clearer.** Counterproductive — it lengthens weak syllables and flattens the exact contrast clarity depends on. If you must slow down, lengthen the *stressed* syllables and the *pauses*; keep weak syllables just as short.
- **Over-articulating.** Four-syllable `comfortable` is wrong, not careful.
- **Suppressing your melody** because someone said you sound sing-song. Don't reduce the movement — **relocate** it.

## Do this today (20 min)

Record it, dated. You can't make this retroactively and you'll want it in six months.

1. Read cold, no prep: *"The development team's report was interesting, though not particularly comfortable reading. It's necessary, I think, to separate the temporary problems from the permanent ones. The laboratory results suggested that the equipment was the difficulty, not the management. Thirteen of the thirty complaints we received last February were about exactly that. Could you check the computer? Is it important?"*
2. Twenty words: *important, development, comfortable, necessary, photography, administration, category, laboratory, employee, determine, image, purchase, separate (adj), separate (verb), thirteen, thirty, greenhouse, green house, record (n), record (v).*
3. Two minutes explaining your job to an outsider. Then one minute on a random news topic, zero prep.

Then four Praat numbers: longest-to-shortest syllable ratio in *administration* · pitch span in English · **pitch span reading French** · speaking rate. That third one is the one nobody takes and the most useful: if your French range is much wider, your problem is **suppression, not capacity**.

Repeat monthly. Don't listen back for three months, then compare and be encouraged.

**Timeline:** work-vocabulary stress, a few weeks (start here, for morale) · monitored speech, 1–3 months · spontaneous speech, 4–8 months of daily work · how people perceive you, 6–18 months.

---

## The part that isn't technical

Doing this properly will feel like pretending, and a wide American pitch range will feel theatrical to a French sensibility. That inhibition is the invisible ceiling on a lot of otherwise diligent practice. Frame it as **a role, not a replacement** — you're adding a register, not deleting your French self. Practice as a character first, privately, deliberately over-the-top; the physical skills transfer and performance mode carries no self-consciousness.

And your target — **completely clear, native-like rhythm, still audibly French** — isn't a compromise. It's the best available goal.
